The heritage

Ronghua, a thousand years before chenille wire

Before it was a slightly retro bit of wire, chenille was a gesture. It comes from China, where silk flowers were made on copper wire to be worn in the hair on the days that matter. This is where we come from.

I

A flower that means “prosperity”

In Chinese, ronghua (绒花) means “velvet flower”. Said aloud, it sounds exactly like ronghua (荣华), “glory and prosperity”. That pun made its fortune: to give or wear a velvet flower was to wish peace and prosperity.

The practice is traced back to the Tang dynasty (618–907). Velvet flowers were sent to the palace as tribute, and court women wore them in their hair in place of fresh flowers, which cannot be had in every season. Under the Ming and the Qing, the custom reached the street: in Nanjing, the quarter between Sanshan Street and Changle Road became the “flower market”, lined with workshops that made and sold them.

They were worn at the three moments of the year that matter: New Year, festivals, weddings. A flower you don't throw away, for a day you don't forget.

II

Three gestures

The material is simple: dyed silk and very fine copper wire, annealed to make it supple. Everything else is in the hand. Nearly ten steps, three of which give the whole craft its name.

Gōu tiáo, “hooking the strand”: the silk is combed, laid between two copper wires, and twisted. Out comes a strand of velvet, supple, that bends any way you like.

Dǎ jiān, “cutting the tip”: with scissors, the strand becomes whatever is needed. Round for a bud, pointed for a leaf, flattened then rounded for a petal.

Chuán huā, “passing on the flower”: with tweezers, the petals are set one by one around a heart, sometimes a pearl is added, and the flower exists. A ten-centimetre piece takes a week of work.

III

From silk to chenille wire

Chenille is French for caterpillar — and, since the 18th century, a velvety trimming yarn made in Lyon and Paris by cutting the fibres of a cloth woven for the purpose. In the early 20th century, industry did the same thing with two steel wires and fibres: the pipe cleaner, still called a chenille stem today.

Look at a pipe cleaner closely: two metal wires twisted around each other, trapping fibres. It is exactly the strand of gōu tiáo, in industrial form. Silk became polyester, copper became steel, the gesture stayed.

Ronghua has always been a workshop craft: silk and copper are costly, and a single piece takes nearly ten steps and a week of work. Almost nobody could ever try it at home. Chenille wire made that velvet strand available to everyone: two twisted wires holding fibres — the same structure as the gōu tiáo strand, for a few cents.

IV

Today

Ronghua nearly vanished with the state workshops of the 1980s. In Nanjing, Zhao Shuxian, who entered a crafts factory at nineteen in 1973, remained one of the last to master the whole craft. He is now its “representative inheritor”, the technique being listed as intangible cultural heritage of Jiangsu province, and he has taken on apprentices.

In 2018, a costume drama, Yanxi Palace, showed nineteen of his headpieces to hundreds of millions of viewers. Overnight, young Chinese women wanted to wear what the empress wore. The little Nanjing workshop has been full ever since.

V

What we keep of it

Our flowers are not ronghua. They are not silk, and they do not come from a Nanjing workshop. They are chenille wire, shaped by hand by artisans who practise the same gestures — press, trim, assemble — then composed into bouquets here, in Paris, in our colours.

A material of today, the old gestures, fewer steps: that is what makes these flowers possible at a price that is no longer a museum's.

What we keep of ronghua is the intention: a flower you don't throw away, given for a day that matters. And a word, chenille, that made the same journey we did — from silk to wire, from China to Paris.
